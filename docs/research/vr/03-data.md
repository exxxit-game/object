## 2. Seated vs standing, and session length

- **Telemetry proxy for "no walking space": SteamVR standing-only (which covers both seated and standing play) 18.5% (Jun 2016) → 25.3% (Feb 2017) → 31% (Nov 2017); the jump came when Rift Touch controllers launched** — Valve 2016 thread (above) — verified yes, full text read yes — "increased by a few points during the week that Rift's motion controls came out" — For us: no source splits seated from standing; a third of PC players in 2017 had no space to walk, so every room must at least work stationary, and walking rooms are an offer, not the default.
- **Expert review: sitting is safer (no falls), more comfortable for long use, more accessible, and tends to reduce cybersickness, but does not stop it; seated users who cannot turn their chair need virtual turning, which can cancel the benefit** — Zielasko & Riecke, "To Sit or Not to Sit in VR: Analyzing Influences and (Dis)Advantages of Posture and Embodied Interaction", 2021, Computers 10(6):73, https://doi.org/10.3390/computers10060073 (Sec. 4.1, 4.2, 4.4, 4.10) — verified yes, full text read yes (Sec. 1–4.4 and 4.10; the expert survey is n = 61) — "simply sitting down does not prevent users from getting sick" — For us: supports offering seated play everywhere and the owner's rule "rooms seated or standing as in the original"; a seated player on a fixed chair will need snap turn, so snap turn must stay available when seated.
- **Old Oculus guidance: remain seated unless the content requires standing; take a 10–15 minute break every 30 minutes; start with a few minutes; about 1 in 4,000 people may have severe dizziness, seizures or blackouts triggered by light flashes** — Oculus VR, "Health & Safety Warnings" (Rift, document 310-30023-01), Feb 2016, https://static.oculus.com/documents/310-30023-01_Rift_HealthSafety_English.pdf — verified yes, full text read yes — "Take at least a 10 to 15 minute break every 30 minutes" — For us: dated (Rift era, and it then said "not for under 13"); the current Quest warnings page only links to per-device documents that could not be read (JS page). Use it as the historical norm: a break offer after about 30 minutes of play (three rooms).
- **Meta (2025): sessions under 15–20 min are seen as less enjoyable than 20+ min sessions; most sessions are under 40 minutes, and enjoyment stops rising after about 40 min; recommended loops 10–20 min and breaks every 10–15 min** — Meta Horizon developer blog, "Building Content to Optimize User Time: 20-40 Minute 'Goldilocks' Session Length on Meta Quest", 24 Jul 2025, https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/optimize-user-time-goldilocks-session-length-meta-quest/ — verified yes (official page, no method or sample given), full text read yes — "most users' sessions are typically under 40 minutes" — For us: a visit of corridor + 2–3 rooms of about 10 min each fits Meta's 20–40 min window; one 10-min room alone is at the short end Meta calls less enjoyable.
- **Representative Norwegian panel (n = 936 in the abstract, 931 in the method; Kantar phone panel, April 2021, adults only): 20% had tried VR, 0.6% use it more than once a week; most who tried it use VR no more than 15 min at a time, under 10% more than 30 min; little gender difference in trying VR, strong drop with age** — Raaen & Eg, "Who uses VR in Norway?", 2021, NIKT (Norsk IKT-konferanse) poster, https://www.ntnu.no/ojs/index.php/nikt/article/view/5223 — verified yes, full text read yes — "use of VR seems to be both infrequent and brief" — For us: casual players (the people a free browser game reaches) play in short bursts; a room must make sense in 10–15 minutes, and the corridor must be quick to pass.
- **HMD owners (n = 227): most use VR up to 2 hours per week on weekdays and up to 2 hours on weekends in total; 67% are the only user of their setup** — Rivu et al. 2021 (above, Sec. 3.3.2, Fig. 1a) — verified yes, full text read yes — "use their setups for up to two hours on weekdays" — For us: enthusiasts, not casuals, still spend only a couple of hours a week; the game competes for short sessions.

## 5. Interpupillary distance

- **Adult IPD: mean about 63 mm; the vast majority of adults are within 50–75 mm; 45–80 mm covers (almost) all adults; children down to age 5 need about 40 mm. In the 1988 ANSUR data (3,976 subjects) IPD ran 52–78 mm, men mean 64.67 (SD 3.71), women 62.31 (SD 3.60); near IPD (converging at 40 cm) is about 3 mm less than far IPD** — Dodgson, "Variation and extrema of human interpupillary distance", 2004, Proc. SPIE 5291, Stereoscopic Displays and Virtual Reality Systems XI, pp. 36–46, https://doi.org/10.1117/12.529999 (author's accepted copy: https://doi.org/10.26686/wgtn.14984190) — verified yes, full text read yes — "mean adult IPD is around 63 mm" — For us: the canonical source; our engine never needs to set IPD (the headset does), but it tells us how many players a headset's lens range leaves out.
- **ANSUR II (2012) IPD by pupillometer: women P5 55.5, P50 62.0, P95 67.5 mm; men P5 58.5, P50 64.0, P95 70.0 mm. [computed from the CSVs] Within Meta's stated 56–70 mm: 93.3% of women, 95.1% of men; below 56 mm: 5.7% of women, 0.9% of men** — Gordon et al. 2014 (measurement 53, Interpupillary Breadth, Hoya digital pupillometer) — verified yes, full text read yes — "measured with a pupillometer" — For us: about 1 woman in 17 has eyes closer than the Quest range; Stanney et al. 2020 (section 7) found IPD misfit, not sex, drives women's higher sickness. Nothing in software fixes it, so content must stay gentle.
- **Meta: Quest headsets fit IPDs of 56–70 mm best, about 95% of adults; lens positions 58 / 63 / 68 mm (for IPD ≤61, 61–66, ≥66 mm)** — Meta Help Center, "Learn about IPD and lens spacing on Meta Quest", https://www.meta.com/help/quest/261777072346131/ (read Oct 2026) — verified yes, full text read yes — "about 95% of adults" — For us: Meta's 95% matches the ANSUR II recomputation (93–95%). The page does not give a Quest 3-specific range.
- **Quest 3 IPD adjustment 53–75 mm (continuous wheel); Quest 2 and 3S only 58 / 63 / 68 mm** — Road to VR, "Quest 3S vs. Quest 3 vs. Quest 2 – Specs Compared", 25 Sep 2024, https://roadtovr.com/quest-3s-quest-3-quest-2-specs-compared/ — verified: press source only (not Meta) — full text read yes (spec table) — no quote — For us: if right, Quest 3 fits about 99% of adults, while Quest 2/3S (three steps) fit fewer; treat as unverified until Meta states it.

## 6. Arm fatigue

- **Shoulder endurance is effectively unlimited only below about 15% of maximum force (Rohmert 1960); the least tiring mid-air posture is the arm bent at the elbow with the hand midway between shoulder and waist (CE 15.55% in that condition, vs higher with the hand at shoulder height or the arm extended); a hand-held button click had the lowest CE of the selection methods (8.18%), dwell was best for one hand; a smaller 25 x 25 cm interaction area tired less than 35 x 35 cm. CE correlated with Borg CR10 exertion (R = 0.846, R² = 0.716; 16 participants)** — Hincapié-Ramos, Guo, Moghadasian & Irani, "Consumed Endurance: A Metric to Quantify Arm Fatigue of Mid-Air Interactions", CHI 2014, https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557130 (author copy http://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/assets/publication_files/Consumed_Endurance_-_CHI_2014.pdf) — verified yes, full text read yes — "The center + bent arm position ... is the least tiring of all positions we tested" — For us: our laser-and-trigger design is the low-fatigue case (bent arm, small wrist motions, a button click); keep answer buttons in the lower part of the sheet, and never require holding the arm up at shoulder height (the sheet 12° below the eyes already does this).
- **In a 20-min VR reading task with controller raycasting, 4 of 8 participants reported arm fatigue, because the controller was held up constantly** — Lee, Healey & Manocha, "VRDoc: Gaze-based Interactions for VR Reading Experience", 2022, arXiv:2211.03001 (ISMAR 2022), https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03001 (Sec. 3.1) — verified yes, full text read yes (copy in the scratchpad, vrdoc.txt) — "Four of the participants reported arm fatigue" — For us: agrees with our notes ("4 of 8 tired within 20 min"); reading must never require the arm to be raised; the sheet waits for the answer, the arm can rest.

## 7. Comfortable viewing, distances and text legibility (helper report)

#### Head rotation and comfort zones

- **Head yaw: comfortable 30° from centre, maximum 55°.** Content zone 77° to each side (DK2 94° FOV/2 + 30°); peripheral zone to 102° (94/2 + 55); behind that is the "curiosity zone". — Alger, M., *Visual Design Methods for Virtual Reality*, MA thesis, Ravensbourne, Sept 2015, http://aperturesciencellc.com/vr/VisualDesignMethodsforVR_MikeAlger.pdf, pp. 37–38 (numbers taken from Chu 2014). — Alger verified yes, full text read yes; Chu's head-rotation slide **not verified** (see "Could not open"). — "comfortably rotate their heads horizontally 30° from the center" — For us: the sheet straight ahead in the player's yaw is well inside the 30° comfortable band.
- **Head pitch: up 20° comfortable / 60° max; down 12° comfortable / 40° max.** — Alger 2015, p. 45, again citing Chu 2014. — Alger verified yes, full text read yes; Chu primary unverified. — "Downwards is less with 12° comfortably and 40° maximum" — For us: Chu's 12° is how far the **head** pitches comfortably. It is **not** a rule for where to place content. Our DROP_DEG = 12 matches the number but not the meaning.
- **Reading area: 15°–50° below eye level, beyond 0.6 m.** Alger slices this from Ankrum (1999, *Visual Ergonomics in the Office*). Alger's VR OS mock-up opens a document "1.3 meters away between 15° and 50° downward". — Alger 2015, pp. 45 and 69. — Alger verified yes, full text read yes; Ankrum 1999 not read (secondary through Alger). — "recommended angle of viewing for longer working periods tends to be between 15°-50° downwards" — For us: desktop-derived. It places reading lower than our 12°.
- **Eyes move comfortably about ±30–35° horizontally and vertically; with the neck the field is about 120°; the comfortable visual centre is about 6° below the horizon. Street View put its UI 2.5 m away, about 6° below the horizon.** — McKenzie, C. & Glazier, A., *Designing Screen Interfaces for VR*, Google I/O 2017, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ES9jArHRFHQ (transcript from the human "English – CC" captions, 18:03–19:30 and 21:30). — verified yes, full text read yes (whole transcript). — "visual center ends up being comfortably around plus six degrees below the horizon" — For us: a VR-specific source puts the reading centre about 6° below the horizon, which is half our 12°.
- **Preferred vertical position of a text box: −1.0 ± 2.6° from horizontal** (n = 18, Oculus Rift CV1, participants set it themselves). Dingler also cites Dix (2009): the eye covers about 60° comfortably, horizontally and vertically, and neck rotation raises that to about 120°. — Dingler, T., Kunze, K. & Outram, B., *VR Reading UIs: Assessing Text Parameters for Reading in VR*, CHI 2018 Extended Abstracts, LBW094, doi:10.1145/3170427.3188695 (author copy https://kaikunze.de/papers/pdf/dingler2018vr.pdf), p. 4 Table 1 and p. 2. — verified yes, full text read yes. — "The preferred vertical position was -1.0 ± 2.6 degrees from the horizontal" — For us: when free to choose, readers put text almost at eye level.
- **Desktop office norm: screen centre 15°–25° below horizontal eye level (§5.2.4.3); 15°–20° below eye level (§7.2.2.2); best gaze angle "about −17° or slightly higher"; whole visual area between 0° and 60° below eye level; eyes do not rotate comfortably more than 30° downward relative to the head during sustained viewing.** — ANSI/HFES 100-2007, *Human Factors Engineering of Computer Workstations*, HFES 2007, §5.2.4.3 (pp. 22–23) and §7.2.2.2 (p. 58). PDF hosted by a vendor: https://www.xybix.com/hubfs/ANSI_HFES_100-200727E2.pdf. — verified yes; full text read **no** (only these clauses). — "the best gaze angle is about -17° or slightly higher" — For us: this is the seated desktop case with a fixed screen. Our 12° sits between this (15°–20°) and the VR sources (−1° to 6°).
- **Viewing "at a glance" without moving the head: a horizontal viewing angle of at most ±15°.** — ISO 9241-306:2018, §5.1.1 (official SIS preview, pp. 2–3). — verified yes; full text read no (preview clauses only). — "maximum horizontal viewing angle of ±15°" — For us: a sheet 0.56 m wide at 1 m spans about ±15.6°, roughly the "at a glance" width.

#### Vergence–accommodation conflict and minimum distance

- **The eye's depth of focus is about ±0.3 D (±0.25 to ±0.3 D); Panum's fusion area is 15–30 arcmin.** — Hoffman, D.M., Girshick, A.R., Akeley, K. & Banks, M.S. (2008), *Vergence–accommodation conflicts hinder visual performance and cause visual fatigue*, Journal of Vision 8(3):33, doi:10.1167/8.3.33 (PMC2879326 author manuscript), sections "Focus cues, fusion, and stereopsis" and "General methods". — verified yes, full text read yes. — "depth of focus, which is roughly ±0.3 diopters (D) under normal circumstances" — For us: a conflict under about 0.3 D lies inside the eye's own focus tolerance.
- **A conflict of ±0.67 D, kept for about 45 min, caused significantly more eye tiredness, blur, eye discomfort and headache than the same task without conflict (11 observers aged 23–31; Wilcoxon p < 0.025). In a pilot, 5 of 11 young adults could not fuse, or found it too tiring to fuse, stimuli with conflicts.** — Hoffman et al. 2008, Experiment 4 and Discussion. — verified yes, full text read yes. — "the first demonstration that mismatches … cause visual fatigue and discomfort" — For us: keep text that is read for long within a small fraction of a diopter of the lens focus.
- **Practical guideline: focus cues have little influence beyond 1 m (Watt et al. 2005). Minimise the conflict by matching the simulated distance to the focal distance.** — Hoffman et al. 2008, "Practical guidelines". — verified yes, full text read yes. — "there should be little influence beyond 1 m" — For us: 1 m is the threshold distance in this rule.
- **Zone of comfort (young adults, 19–34): far boundary Df = 1.129·Dv + 0.442; near boundary Df = 1.035·Dv − 0.626 (Df = focal distance, Dv = vergence distance, both in D).   | Headset focal distance | Near comfort limit | Far comfort limit | Conflict at our 1 m |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1.3 m (Quest, Quest 2) | 0.74 m | 3.45 m | 0.23 D |
  | 1.5 m (if Quest 3 were 1.5 m) | 0.80 m | 5.0 m | 0.33 D |
  | 2.0 m (older headsets, per Carmack) | 0.92 m | 19.5 m | 0.50 D |

## 8. VR sickness: incidence, sex and IPD, age, time course, predictors, SSQ (helper report)

### Incidence

- **Pooled post-exposure SSQ total in HMDs = 28.00 (95% CI 24.66-31.35). The pool is 55 papers with 3,016 participants: pooled mean age 24, 41% female. Study means ranged from 14.30 to 35.27** — Saredakis D, Szpak A, Birckhead B, Keage HAD, Rizzo A, Loetscher T, "Factors Associated With Virtual Reality Sickness in Head-Mounted Displays: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis", 2020, Front. Hum. Neurosci. 14:96, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2020.00096 (abstract p.1; Table 3 p.9; p.9 text) — verified yes, full text read yes — "the pooled total SSQ mean was relatively high 28.00" — For us: a typical HMD study participant ends at about SSQ 28. Our rooms should aim well below that, and we should treat 28 as "ordinary VR", not as "safe".

- **Dropout because of VR sickness: mean 15.6% across 46 experiments. Only studies that reported dropouts were counted, so the authors say the rate may be inflated** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (p.5 "Dropouts"; p.13 limitations) — verified yes, full text read yes — "the rate of 15.6% may be inflated" — For us: in lab studies about 1 in 6 people quit. At home there is nobody to stop them, so an "I feel unwell / stop" exit must be available at every moment.

- **SSQ total by content type: gaming 34.26, 360 video 27.42, minimalist 21.71, scenic 17.33 (pooled means). Gaming studies with the highest dropouts ranged from 44 to 100% (secondary: Merhi 2007, Dennison 2016, Munafo 2017)** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (Table 3 p.9; Discussion p.10) — verified yes, full text read yes — "gaming content recorded the highest total SSQ mean 34.26" — For us: we are "gaming content". Low visual motion and scenic or minimal rooms sit at the low end.

- **Rollercoaster content: almost 67% could not finish 14 min (Nesbitt 2017). Withdrawal in rollercoaster studies was up to 92.86% (Nesbitt 2017, Nalivaiko 2015, Gavgani 2017)** — Saredakis et al. 2020 p.2 and Caserman P, Garcia-Agundez A, Gámez Zerban A, Göbel S, "Cybersickness in current-generation virtual reality head-mounted displays: systematic review and outlook", 2021, Virtual Reality 25:1153-1170, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10055-021-00513-6 (p.1160) — verified yes as secondary (both reviews read in full; the originals were not read) — "a withdrawal rate of up to 92.86%" — For us: never move the player's viewpoint for them (no carts, rides or forced camera motion). This is the most sickening class of content.

- **60-95% of participants feel some cybersickness during exposure, and 6-12.9% end exposure early (secondary: Stanney 2003, Arns & Cerney 2005, Roberts & Gallimore 2005, Regan 1995, Kim 2005)** — Caserman et al. 2021 (p.1153) — verified yes as secondary; originals not read — "60-95% of participants experience some level of CS" — For us: most players will feel something. Our goal is "mild and brief", not "zero".

- **Matched vs mismatched visual and body motion: mean SSQ 12.78 (matched; n=203) vs 31.84 (mismatched; n=667), p<0.0001. Dropout averaged 3.21% (matched) vs 7.11% (mismatched)** — Caserman et al. 2021 (p.1160, Table 2, Table 6) — verified yes, full text read yes — "on average, 7.11% of the subjects dropped out" — For us: when the player's real head and body movement drives all view motion, sickness is about one third of mismatched content. Keep the camera locked to the head inside rooms.

- **By headset: HTC Vive with positional tracking had SSQ 13.98 vs Oculus Rift DK1 33.94 and DK2 31.97 (p<0.0001). Withdrawal was DK1 24.50% vs DK2 9.60%. Vive withdrawals were matched 2.67% and mismatched 1.81%. Rift CV1 mismatched studies (n=140) had 0.00% withdrawal and SSQ 33.75** — Caserman et al. 2021 (p.1155; Table 1 summary rows; Table 6) — verified yes, full text read yes — "last-generation HMD devices have significantly fewer problems with CS" — For us: Quest 3 is far newer than these headsets. Modern tracking lowers sickness, but content mismatch still dominates.

- **Locomotion: controller-based travel had total SSQ 32.55, stationary 28.04, physical walking 16.99 (pooled). Note that Saredakis counts teleporting as "controller"** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (Methods p.4; Table 3 p.9; p.12) — verified yes, full text read yes — "controller-based movement displaying the highest total SSQ mean (M = 32.55)" — For us: real walking within the guardian is the lowest-sickness way to move. Teleport is grouped with "controller" in this pool, so use the per-technique numbers below.

- **Teleport vs smooth locomotion, same study: teleport SSQ 17.50 vs gaze-directed 25.40 vs pointing (steering) 36.40 (Christou & Aristidou 2017, <10 min, n=18). In Frommel 2017: fixpoint teleport 23.38, free teleport 21.97, guided 35.84, touchpad 38.34** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (Table 1 p.6) and Caserman et al. 2021 (Table 4 p.1164) — verified yes as secondary (the two reviews agree; originals not read) — "discrete locomotion techniques, such as teleportation" (Caserman p.1165) — For us: teleport in the corridor is the right choice. ### Sex and IPD fit

- **After a 20-min provocative virtual rollercoaster (HTC Vive), mean SSQ was 52.11 for women vs 24.93 for men (more than 2x, p=0.05). At 1 h after: women 31.42 vs men 3.24 (p=0.012). On a flat TV the same content gave women 7.95 and men 5.61** — Stanney K, Fidopiastis C, Foster L, "Virtual Reality Is Sexist: But It Does Not Have to Be", 2020, Front. Robot. That is longer than one of our rooms.

- **IPD non-fit is the main driver of the sex difference: 5 of 15 women (33.3%) vs 0 of 15 men had an IPD outside the Vive's 60.5-74.4 mm range. - **When the IPD fits, there is no sex difference. ### Age

- **Older samples (mean age 35 or more; 4 studies, n=64) had total SSQ 14.30 vs 28.44 for younger samples (Q=7.430, p=0.006). Disorientation was lower in older samples, while nausea and oculomotor differences were not significant. The evidence base is small, and three of the four older studies used scenic, often treadmill-walking content** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (abstract; Table 2-3 p.8-9; "Age and VR Sickness" p.12) — verified yes, full text read yes — "these findings are based on a small evidence base" — For us: older players are probably not at higher risk in calm HMD content, but the data are thin.

- **HMD (HTC Vive), seated on a swivel chair, teleport-only travel, four experiences totalling 18 min. Older adults (n=20, mean 71.3 y) had unweighted SSQ 1.35 (SD 1.60) vs younger adults (n=20, mean 19.7 y) 3.50 (SD 3.22), p<.05. Sickness was low in both groups** — Dilanchian AT, Andringa R, Boot WR, "A Pilot Study Exploring Age Differences in Presence, Workload, and Cybersickness in the Experience of Immersive Virtual Reality Environments", 2021, Front. - **At home on own Quest, ages 18-65 (n=263): each extra year of age lowered the SSQ change by 0.65 points (95% CI −1.09 to −0.21, p=0.004). Mean FMS showed the same direction (b = −0.010, p=0.04). ### Time course and session length

- **In most studies sickness rises with exposure time, but there is no universal maximum safe time. Some studies show a plateau or a decline after a point** — Dużmańska N, Strojny P, Strojny A, "Can Simulator Sickness Be Avoided? A Review on Temporal Aspects of Simulator Sickness", 2018, Front. Psychol. 9:2132, https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02132 (abstract p.1; Results p.7-8; Conclusion p.11) — verified yes, full text read yes — "severity of the simulator sickness symptoms increases with time of exposure" — For us: room length is a real dose. About 10 minutes per room is a reasonable cap with breaks between rooms, but no source gives a hard number for our content.

- **Onset around 10 minutes: in a 60-min driving simulation the first significant SSQ rise appeared at 10 min. Nausea and disorientation appeared after 10 min and oculomotor symptoms after 25 min (Min 2004, secondary). Feenstra 2011 found differences significant after 10 min of a 20-min trial (secondary)** — Dużmańska et al. 2018 (p.6-7) — verified yes as secondary — "appeared 10 min after beginning of the trial" — For us: rooms of about 10 minutes sit right at the usual onset point. Avoid stacking two provocative rooms back to back without a calm break.

- **Plateau around 9 minutes: in five 2-min HMD trials, SSQ after trial 5 exceeded the earlier trials but not trial 4. The authors infer the peak is reached after about 9 min (Moss 2008, secondary)** — Dużmańska et al. 2018 (p.7) — verified yes as secondary — "after circa 9 min of exposure the simulator sickness has reached its peak" — For us: in low-motion content the dose may level off around room length. We should measure our own rooms rather than assume.

- **Cybersickness usually appears about 10-15 min after immersion, and symptoms usually go away about 15 min after leaving VR. Re-entering can bring them back abruptly (secondary: DiZio & Lackner 1997, 2000; Lampton 1994)** — Caserman et al. 2021 (p.1153) — verified yes as secondary — "CS usually appears approximately 10-15 min after immersion" — For us: a player who felt unwell in room N should get a real break (minutes, with the headset off) before room N+1, not an instant restart.

- **SSQ by exposure category (pooled): under 10 min 23.47, 10 min or more 33.42, 20 min or more 27.35. Time was not significant for total SSQ (Q=5.433, p=0.066), but nausea (p<0.001) and disorientation (p=0.028) were lower under 10 min. The 10-min group held the most gaming content (62%)** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (Methods p.4; Table 2 p.8; Table 3 p.9; "VR Exposure Time" p.12) — verified yes, full text read yes — "scores were lower for studies that were equal to or >20 min" — For us: content type confounds duration. Keeping each room under about 10 minutes helps mainly with nausea and dizziness.

- **Dose over a single session (between-subject, controller travel): SSQ 19.96 at 15 min, 25.73 at 30, 29.08 at 45, 32.10 at 60 (Stanney 2003 via Saredakis Table 1). [computed] That is about +0.27 SSQ per minute on average, slowing from about 0.38/min (15-30 min) to about 0.20/min (45-60 min)** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (Table 1 p.7) — verified yes as secondary; original not read — no quote — For us: the climb is fastest early. A long play session made of several rooms is the main dose, so offer a break after every room or two.

- **Recovery depends on how sick the person got: those with severe symptoms (SSQ above 60) needed more than 30 min, while those at SSQ 25 or less recovered within 5 min (Tanaka & Takagi 2004, secondary). Across studies, aftereffects lasted from about 10 min to as long as 4 h** — Dużmańska et al. 2018 (p.10-11) — verified yes as secondary — "total SSQ score of 25 or less" — For us: keeping players below roughly 25 should mean they are fine within minutes after taking the headset off. That matters at home, where people may stand up, walk on stairs or drive afterwards.

- **Recovery in a deliberately sickening 10-min projected-screen stimulus: sickness ratings fell roughly exponentially to full recovery in most people by 5 min and in all by 10 min. 17% stopped early at moderate nausea** — Golding, Rafiq & Keshavarz 2021 (p.6; Table 3 p.6) — verified yes, full text read yes — "full subjective recovery in most participants by 5 min and in all by 10 min" — For us: after a room ends, a short calm interval (lobby, no motion) lets most players recover.

- **Adaptation across sessions (helicopter trainer, 3,691 hops): the 75th-percentile total score was 15 on hop 1 and close to zero by hop 6. Most sickness had subsided after 4 hops. Sickness was highest when hops were on the same day or 1 day apart, and the best spacing was 2-5 days (Figures 3-4)** — Kennedy et al. 1993 (p.216-218; Fig. 3-4 read from page images) — verified yes, full text read yes — no quote — For us: players adapt over repeated visits. Spreading rooms over separate days (2-5 days apart) is the classic recommendation if a player is sensitive.

- **Repeated short exposures beat one long one: in Kennedy 2000 (53 pilots, 7 sessions) the drop in sickness over trials outweighed the rise from a longer single exposure. In Newman 2013 adaptation happened mostly between sessions 1 and 2 and was still present on day 22. Reinhard 2017 says the first immersion needs extra caution (shorter trials, longer rests). All secondary** — Dużmańska et al. 2018 (p.8-9) — verified yes as secondary — "the first VR immersion should be treated with extreme caution" — For us: the corridor and the first room are the first immersion for most players. They should be the calmest part of the game.

- **Stanney's habituation recipe (secondary, Welch 2014): active rather than passive control, immediate feedback, incremental rather than massed exposure with stimulus strength rising step by step, and 2-5 day gaps. Habituation is "perhaps as high as 85% effective" (Benson 1999, secondary)** — Stanney et al. 2020 (p.14) — verified yes as secondary — "(e.g., 2-5 day intersession intervals)" — For us: if any room must be more provocative, place it later and keep earlier rooms calm. Player-driven motion is better than passive motion.

### Susceptibility predictors

- **Past motion-sickness history (MHQ) is the second driver after IPD fit. Experiment 1 regression: recovery SSQ = −2.51 + 47.45·IPD-fit + 4.66·MHQ (R² = .469). Combined data (n=135): IPD fit 8.22 (p=.022), MHQ 2.62 (p<.001), exposure duration −2.20 (dropouts), bradygastria 0.421 (R² = .322). Prior VR and gaming experience were not significant (p = .770 and .098)** — Stanney et al. 2020 (Table 6 p.9; Table 10 p.12) — verified yes, full text read yes — "motion sickness history a secondary driver" — For us: a one-line pre-game question ("do you get car- or sea-sick?") picks out the higher-risk players without measuring anything.

- **6-item VIMSSQ-short (nausea, headache, dizziness, fatigue, eyestrain with screens, plus an "avoided devices" item; each scored 0-3, total 0-18). It correlated r = .58 with post-exposure SSQ and alone predicted 34% of variance; adding MSSQ, migraine, syncope and dizziness impact raised this to adjusted R² .56. Cronbach's alpha was .84 and the sample mean 4.9 (SD 4.1). Age, gender and vection did not predict, but the age range was narrow** — Golding, Rafiq & Keshavarz 2021 (abstract; Table 1 p.3; Table 2 p.6; Table 4 p.7) — verified yes, full text read yes — "the VIMSSQ predicted 34% of the individual variation of VIMS" — For us: the best-validated short pre-play screen for screen and VR sickness. It is six questions that could fit on one sheet before room 1, but it is a research tool, not a medical test.

- **VIMSSQ (long form) norms (n=322): total median 22.0 (P75 41.95, P90 66.0). Its correlation with post-exposure SSQ was r = .60, while "avoidance of devices because of symptoms" alone correlated r = .69. VIMSSQ explained 35.4% of SSQ variance alone, rising to adjusted 59% with avoidance and syncope. 29.5% of respondents said symptoms made them stop or cut down some device, VR 2.8%** — Keshavarz et al. 2023 (Table 1; Results "Symptom Frequency..."; Table 6; Part 2 Results) — verified yes, full text read yes — "caused them to stop (or significantly reduce) the use" — For us: the single strongest question is "have screen or VR symptoms ever made you avoid a device?" A "yes" means start that player on the calmest settings.

- **At home on own Quest (n=263): VIMSSQ-nausea predicted higher in-ride FMS (b=0.026, p=0.005). Expecting to get sick was the strongest and most consistent factor (+11.4 SSQ change, 95% CI 5.7-17.2, p<0.001). The recruitment text warned that the study induces sickness, and the authors relate this to a nocebo effect (Mao 2021, secondary: positively framed warnings removed the nocebo effect). VR familiarity showed only a weak trend (p=0.09)** — Gottsacker et al. 2026, arXiv:2609.26203 (Sec. V-E to V-G p.9-10; Discussion p.12) — verified yes (preprint), full text read yes — no quote — For us: how we word the comfort warning matters. A neutral or positively framed notice ("most people feel fine; stop any time") is better than a scary one, and it must still be honest.

- **Motion sickness susceptibility is about 55-70% heritable (secondary: Reavley 2006 twin studies). The MSSQ-short population norm is a mean of 12.9 (SD 9.9), and percentile 50 is the norm by definition (secondary: Golding 2006)** — Golding, Rafiq & Keshavarz 2021 (Introduction p.2; Table 2 p.6) — verified yes as secondary — "heritability estimates of 55-70%" — For us: some players will get sick whatever we do. The design goal is a safe exit and low dose, not elimination.

### SSQ (Kennedy 1993)

- **Origin: built from 1,119 pre/post pairs of the Pensacola Motion Sickness Questionnaire collected at 10 US Navy flight simulators. 12 of 28 symptoms were dropped (too rare or misleading, e.g. boredom, drowsiness, vomiting), leaving 16. Factor analysis gave 3 clusters: Nausea, Oculomotor, Disorientation** — Kennedy et al. 1993 (p.205-207; Table 1) — verified yes, full text read yes (scan; OCR text has errors, tables read from page images) — no quote — For us: the SSQ was normed on fit male military pilots in simulators, not on gamers in HMDs.

- **The 16 items and their subscales (each item rated 0-3; Table 4 p.212):**
  - General discomfort: N, O
  - Fatigue: O
  - Headache: O
  - Eyestrain: O
  - Difficulty focusing: O, D
  - Increased salivation: N
  - Sweating: N
  - Nausea: N, D
  - Difficulty concentrating: N, O
  - Fullness of head: D
  - Blurred vision: O, D
  - Dizzy (eyes open): D
  - Dizzy (eyes closed): D
  - Vertigo: D
  - Stomach awareness: N
  - Burping: N

  Each subscale has 7 items, and 5 items count in two subscales — Kennedy et al. 1993 (Table 4 p.212) — verified yes, full text read yes — no quote — For us: if we ever collect SSQ (e.g. testers), use exactly these 16 items and this mapping.

- **Scoring formula: N = [sum of N items] × 9.54; O = [sum of O items] × 7.58; D = [sum of D items] × 13.92; Total Score = ([N sum] + [O sum] + [D sum]) × 3.74. The constants make each scale's SD 15 in the calibration sample, and "no symptoms" scores 0. Subscale maxima are N 200.34, O 159.18, D 292.32 (stated in Dużmańska p.3, and they match the formula). [computed] Total maximum = 63 × 3.74 = 235.62. Dużmańska's stated total range of 0-179.52 counts each item once and does not match Kennedy's formula** — Kennedy et al. 1993 (p.211-212, Table 4); Dużmańska et al. 2018 (p.3) — verified yes, full text read yes (both) — "total score can range from 0 to 179.52" (Dużmańska; this conflicts with Kennedy) — For us: implement TS from the three raw subscale sums. A unit test should check that the all-3 answer gives 235.62.

- **A common error in the literature: some authors multiply the already-weighted subscale scores by 3.74, inflating totals. Saredakis excluded or corrected such papers** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (p.3) — verified yes, full text read yes — "multiplying the weighted subscale scores by 3.74 thereby producing inflated total scores" — For us: this is a guard-worthy mistake in any scoring code.

- **Calibration norms (about 1,100 post-exposure observations, 10 simulators): Total Score mean 9.8, SD 15.0, maximum 108.6. Percentiles of TS: 50th = 3.7, 75th = 15.0, 90th = 30.0, 95th = 44.9, 99th = 75.9. Subscale means N 7.7, O 10.6, D 6.4. A score of zero accounts for at least 40% and up to 75% of observations depending on the scale** — Kennedy et al. 1993 (Table 5 p.214; p.213) — verified yes, full text read yes — no quote — For us: a total around 15 is the 75th percentile of the original simulator population. The pooled HMD mean of 28 (Saredakis) would sit near the 90th percentile of Kennedy's simulator users.

- **Per-simulator means: the most sickening Navy simulator (2F64C, SH-3 helicopter) had TS 18.8 and the least (2F112, F-14) 1.5. Kennedy gives NO fixed cut-offs. New simulators are compared to these ranges, and a mean in the range of the upper three or four simulators warrants closer examination** — Kennedy et al. 1993 (Table 6 p.214; p.212-213) — verified yes, full text read yes — no quote — For us: the frequently quoted "SSQ > 20 = bad simulator" rule is NOT in the 1993 paper.

- **The commonly cited categories: under 5 negligible, 5-10 minimal, 10-15 significant, 15-20 concerning, over 20 "bad simulator". Caserman attributes them to Stanney et al. 1997 and Saredakis to Kennedy et al. 2003; neither original was read here. Caserman proposes 40 as the new "bad" line for current HMDs, because SSQ 40 or more went with about one-third withdrawal in their corpus** — Caserman et al. 2021 (p.1154; p.1165); Saredakis et al. 2020 (p.10) — verified yes as secondary (categories); the 40 proposal is verified (Caserman's own) — "we suggest 40 would be a more precise value" — For us: use these bands only with the "secondary" label. With today's HMD content, the 20-point line is crossed by ordinary use.

- **The SSQ is meant for post-exposure use, and only for people screened as in their "usual state of fitness". Kennedy 1993 says it is not for pre-exposure baselines. Stanney 2020 excluded anyone with baseline SSQ above 12** — Kennedy et al. 1993 (p.206-207, p.211); Stanney et al. 2020 (Procedure p.4) — verified yes, full text read yes — "participants that scored > 12 were thanked ... and excluded" (Stanney) — For us: tired, ill or hungover players start "sick" before our game does anything.

- **The SSQ baseline is not zero in the general public at home. With no VR at all (online survey, n=93, ages 18-80), the unweighted total was mean 9.66 (SD 7.11), median 8 (IQR 4-14.5). Only 3 people (3.2%) scored zero. Scored with Kennedy weights, 73.1% would already count as a "bad intervention" (over 20) and 58.1% would exceed 40. Healthy and medical subgroups did not differ (p=0.869)** — Brown P, Spronck P, Powell W, "The simulator sickness questionnaire, and the erroneous zero baseline assumption", 2022, Front. Virtual Real. 3:945800, https://doi.org/10.3389/frvir.2022.945800 (Results p.5; Table 3-4 p.6-7) — verified yes, full text read yes — "only 3 (3.2%) of the 93 participants reporting a zero baseline" — For us: any comfort measurement of our rooms (testers, telemetry) must take a before and after reading and report the change, never the after score alone.

- **HMD symptom profile: disorientation is the largest pooled subscale (23.50), then oculomotor (17.09) and nausea (16.72). Classic motion sickness has nausea first. In home Quest users disorientation was also the largest subscale (72.2 vs nausea 56.0 vs oculomotor 32.9)** — Saredakis et al. 2020 (p.10); Gottsacker et al. 2026 (Table II p.8; p.7) — verified yes, full text read yes — "highest pooled SSQ subscale scores for disorientation (M = 23.50)" — For us: players are most likely to report dizziness and vertigo, not vomiting. Room design should avoid anything that shifts the horizon or the floor.

- **Research-method norms that also apply to testing our game: sample sizes above 15 users (Kennedy & Fowlkes 1992, secondary) and immersion of at least 20 min (Wilson 1996, secondary). Early signs of sickness are atypical eye movements, sweating and fiddling with the headset (Cobb 1999, secondary)** — Caserman et al. 2021 (p.1165) — verified yes as secondary — "immersion times should be of at least 20 min" — For us: one owner session in the headset cannot show comfort. A comfort claim needs about 15+ testers, which we do not have, so we must not claim "comfortable" as a measured fact.

## Top 15 for our game (ranked)

1. **Most players have no walking space; on Quest the default boundary is Stationary.** Meta auto-creates a 1 x 1 m Stationary boundary; SteamVR 2017: 31% standing-only, only 52–69% had 1.8 x 1.8 m; Beat Saber 2023: 45.6% under 4 m². Every room and the corridor must work stationary; walking rooms are offers (Meta help; Valve 2016–17; Nair 2023).
2. **Teleport roughly halves sickness compared with stick steering in the same scene** (SSQ 17.5 vs 36.4; free teleport 22.0 vs touchpad 38.3), and matched head-driven motion gives about a third of mismatched content's SSQ (12.8 vs 31.8) (Saredakis 2020; Caserman 2021). Keeps teleport + snap as default.
3. **Never move the player's view for them.** On their own Quest at home, half of players hit the stop level within about 4 minutes of passive forced motion, and almost none quit on their own (Gottsacker 2026, preprint); rollercoaster content loses up to 93% (Caserman 2021). Always-on exit; no rides, carts or camera moves.
4. **IPD fit, not sex, drives the higher sickness of women.** About 6% of women (ANSUR II: 5.7%) have IPDs below Quest's 56 mm; misfit women stayed sick an hour after a 20-min ride (Stanney 2020; Gordon 2014; Meta IPD page). No software fix: keep all content gentle and remind players to set the lens wheel.
5. **The seated threshold 1.35 m misclassifies children.** Meta allows players from age 10 and teens are its most active group; a median 10-year-old's eyes are about 1.31 m (NHANES; Pruett 2025; Family Center). Needs an owner decision (see Conflicts).
6. **Sheet at 1 m is right for the optics.** Quest/Quest 2 focus at 1.3 m puts the comfort zone at 0.74–3.45 m; at 1 m the focus conflict (0.23 D) is inside the eye's depth of focus (Shibata 2011; Hoffman 2008; Carmack 2021; Meta Display).
7. **Rooms of about 10 minutes sit at the usual sickness onset (10–15 min); keep each room under about 10 minutes and offer a real break after 2–3 rooms** (Caserman 2021; Dużmańska 2018; Oculus 2016: 10–15 min break every 30 min; Meta 2025: sessions mostly under 40 min).
8. **Low arm, bent elbow, button click: the least tiring way to answer** (Hincapié-Ramos 2014); in a 20-min controller reading task half the people got arm fatigue (VRDoc 2022). The sheet below eye level answered by trigger is the right design.
9. **Answer targets: Meta's floor is 2.5–3°, Google's 3.67° plus padding; our 4.0° buttons pass both** (Meta Hands UI; McKenzie & Glazier 2017).
10. **Letter size: comfortable reading is about 0.6–1.1° cap height on Quest 2 (2–3x the measured 0.31–0.38° threshold), Google body text 1.375° font size; our minimum is now Google's 24 dmm, a 1.375° font size (MIN_LETTER)** (Kilpeläinen 2023; McKenzie & Glazier 2017; Kojic 2020).
11. **Young adults are the most sickness-prone in HMDs; older adults are less, not more** (Saredakis 2020: 14.3 vs 28.4; Dilanchian 2021; Gottsacker 2026: −0.65 SSQ per year of age). Children (peak susceptibility about 9–10 years, secondary) are a separate risk.
12. **A one-question screen works: "Have screen or VR symptoms ever made you avoid a device?" (r = .69 with sickness), or the 6-item VIMSSQ-short (34% of variance)** (Keshavarz 2023; Golding 2021). Candidate for a comfort question before room 1.
13. **If we ever measure comfort: Kennedy's SSQ formula (total = raw N+O+D × 3.74, max 235.62), always before and after — the public's no-VR baseline is not zero (73% would already be "bad" over 20)** (Kennedy 1993; Brown 2022). The "over 20 = bad simulator" rule is not in Kennedy 1993.
14. **The audience is about two thirds Quest on PC (Steam, Sept 2026: Quest 3 26.9%, Quest 2 25.8%, 3S 11.1%), about one third women among owners, three in four with some past VR discomfort, mostly eyestrain** (Valve 2026; Kelly 2021).
15. **Casual players play briefly: most who tried VR use it 15 min or less at a time; Meta finds sessions under 15–20 min less enjoyable** (Raaen & Eg 2021; Meta 2025). The corridor must be quick; a visit of 2–3 rooms is the natural session.
